# Series Explainer

## The Original Spark

The idea started in the Ando prep as an event/content format:

**"What's Your AI Tech Stack?"**

The first version was meant to be a simple prompt in any format:

> What tools are you actually using, in what order, and for what?

It could work as an icebreaker at a dinner, a recurring Notion page, a social post series, or a fireside warm-up.

The cool part was that it made AI adoption specific and personal instead of abstract. Not "what should people use?" but:

> What's on your desktop right now? What's open? What do you actually reach for?

The answer reveals how people are actually working with AI, not how they think they should be.

That became the bigger question:

What if "what's in my AI tech stack?" looked less like a SaaS list and more like a Glossier bathroom cabinet?

Not a productivity thread. Not a listicle. Not "top 10 AI tools." More like:

- what do you actually reach for every day?
- what is always open?
- what did you buy into?
- what did you quit?
- what is weirdly intimate?
- what makes you feel more capable?
- what does your desktop say about you?

The fun of it was that AI tools have started to feel like personal objects. People have preferences, loyalties, guilty pleasures, toxic exes, daily drivers, and emotional support tabs.

## Where The Note First Appeared

This note first showed up while Saachi was prepping for Ando, but the series is not about Ando.

The prep context helped surface the idea because Saachi was thinking about events, community, and category language:

- movements need shared vocabulary
- events build community
- community builds vocabulary
- vocabulary builds the movement

The useful line from that prep was that events are "the room where the language gets made."

That is why the format felt useful to Saachi. A question like "what's on your desktop?" is not just cute. It gives people a way to talk about AI-native work without defaulting to vague words like "AI tools," "copilots," or "automation."

It makes the category visible through actual behavior:

- what is open
- what gets used first
- what stays in the workflow
- what replaced something else
- what feels embarrassing but useful
- what people are copying from each other

The original work-event version was:

Ask a founder, an ops lead, or an engineer what is on their desktop right now. The answer reveals how they are actually working with agents, not how they think they should be.

For Saachi's series, the company frame falls away. The personal version is better:

Ask a person what is on their desktop. The answer reveals how they are actually arranging intelligence, support, memory, taste, and ambition around themselves.

Saachi is the first subject because this started as her own question about her own tools, her own desk, and her own little systems.

## What Felt Cool About It

The cool part was the collision of references:

### Glossier Top Shelf, but software

The Top Shelf works because it is a recurring profile format built around daily-use inventory. A cleanser or lip balm can say something about taste, routine, aspiration, and identity.

AI tools are starting to work the same way. ChatGPT, Claude, Perplexity, Cursor, PowerPoint, NotebookLM, Notion, Granola, personal scripts, and weird private prompts all have a "daily reach" quality.

### Toy Stories, but for adults and their little machines

Gabriele Galimberti's *Toy Stories* works because it shows a person with the objects that reveal their world.

This series asks: what if the toys are tabs, apps, prompts, agents, dashboards, automations, folders, and screenshots?

### In Her Kitchen, but for tools people made useful

Galimberti's grandmother/food work adds another layer: not just person plus object, but person plus a thing made with care, memory, and use.

That matters here because the best AI stack stories are not only about tools someone downloaded. They are about systems someone built because they needed them: a job dashboard, a screenshot skill, an adventure planner, a learning log, a prompt habit, a dashboard sync.

The object is interesting because of the story behind it.

### Myspace Top 8, but for tools

Ranking tools is funny because it is not neutral. Your Top 8 says what you depend on, what you want to be seen as, and what you are embarrassed to admit.

"Daily Driver," "Emotional Support Tab," "Toxic Ex," and "Paid For Reluctantly" make tool choice feel personal instead of sterile.

### Desk setup tour, but with AI

People already understand that a desk setup is a self-portrait: monitors, notebooks, mugs, cables, wallpaper, tabs, wall art, and all the little objects that make a working life feel like yours.

AI adds a new layer to that portrait. The desktop now shows not only how someone works, but how they think with machines.

## The Actual Premise

**What's On My Desktop** is a visual essay series about AI adoption as material culture.

It looks at people through their desktops, desk setups, AI tools, tabs, prompts, wallpapers, screenshots, objects, and digital rituals.

The series asks:

- what do you use?
- what do you use it for?
- when did it enter your life?
- who did you learn from?
- what did you copy?
- what did you build because you needed it?
- what still feels human?

## Why It Is Not Just A Tech Stack Interview

A normal tech stack interview asks what tools someone uses.

This asks what the tools reveal.

The point is not to recommend the best AI apps. The point is to show how a person has arranged intelligence, memory, taste, ambition, anxiety, and support around themselves.

It is also low-lift, high-authenticity, and shareable: a real desktop, real screenshots, real tools, real dates, real origin stories.

## The Intellectual Frame

AI adoption is usually described with abstract language: productivity, automation, leverage, agents, copilots, transformation.

But people do not use AI abstractly. They use it as material culture:

- tabs
- folders
- screenshots
- subscriptions
- prompts
- dashboards
- browser extensions
- local files
- recurring rituals
- tools copied from other people

The desktop is where all of that becomes visible.

This series treats the desktop as evidence. What remains open, what gets paid for, what gets abandoned, what gets copied, and what becomes infrastructure all reveal how a person is actually adapting to AI.

The series is interested in AI not as a technology category, but as a set of lived arrangements.

## Why People Will Look

People are voyeuristic.

They want to see the real setup: the tabs, the wallpaper, the dock, the folders, the subscriptions, the half-broken systems, the tool someone secretly loves, the object on the desk that explains more than the bio does.

That is why the series should be artifact-led. Do not only ask someone to describe their stack. Show the desk. Show the screenshots. Show the receipts. Show the timeline. Let the reader feel like they are being allowed to peek inside a working life.

The voyeurism should be consensual and tender, not gotcha. The point is not to expose someone. The point is to reveal the small, specific evidence of how they actually work.

The strongest pieces will make readers think:

- I want to see the rest of her tabs.
- I want to know why that tool is still open.
- I want to know what she pays for.
- I want to know what she copied.
- I want to know what her system says about her.

## Why It Is Useful

It is hard to know what is actually useful and what is hype.

Most AI content is either too abstract, too sponsored, too founder-coded, or too clean. It tells people what they should use, not what someone actually kept using after the novelty wore off.

This series is useful because it shows survival:

- what stayed in the daily workflow
- what became a habit
- what got abandoned
- what moved from experiment to infrastructure
- what someone pays for
- what feels overhyped but still useful
- what only works because of the person's specific context

The question is not "is this tool good?"

The better question is:

> What kind of person, doing what kind of work, would actually keep reaching for this?

That is the practical value of the format. It makes usefulness visible through behavior.

## Why Start With Saachi

Saachi's own stack is a good pilot because it is not just "I use ChatGPT."

It has a visible arc:

- thinking partner
- skill-building
- dashboard
- job-search system
- screenshot skill
- automation hooks
- learning log
- AI stack that starts tracking itself

It also has a real desk: wall notes, adventure references, handwritten reminders, a mountain wallpaper, multiple screens, and physical objects that make the digital system feel emotionally legible.

## Why Women, Maybe

The series may be especially interesting through women because the public story of AI adoption is often loud, abstract, and founder-coded.

This format lets AI fluency look different:

- intimate
- practical
- visually specific
- emotionally honest
- copied and remixed
- built around real life
- not pretending to be frictionless

It can show women using AI not as hype, but as leverage, support, taste, career infrastructure, creative confidence, and self-authored systems.

## The Core Line

A desktop is a self-portrait. An AI stack is one layer of it.
